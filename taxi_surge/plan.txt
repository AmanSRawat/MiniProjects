  Industry-Level Project Blueprint for Taxi Operations Analytics

  ---
  1. PROBLEM UNDERSTANDING

  Dataset Representation & Business Context

  The @sigma_cabs.csv dataset almost certainly represents operational taxi trip 
  records from a cab service (likely similar to NYC TLC or Uber/Lyft trip data).
   Each row corresponds to a single completed taxi trip with attributes
  capturing:
  - Temporal metadata: Pickup/drop-off timestamps
  - Spatial metadata: Pickup/drop-off coordinates (longitude/latitude) or zone
  IDs
  - Trip characteristics: Passenger count, trip distance/duration, rate type
  - Financial metrics: Fare components (base fare, tolls, tips, taxes,
  surcharges)
  - Operational details: Vendor ID, payment type, store-and-forward flag

  Likely Business Objectives

  Based on standard taxi industry priorities, this dataset would support:
  - Dynamic pricing optimization: Predict optimal fare multipliers during high
  demand
  - Demand forecasting: Anticipate trip volume by time/location for fleet
  positioning
  - Route efficiency: Identify profitable corridors or congestion-prone areas
  - Driver performance: Analyze earnings patterns tied to behavior or external
  factors
  - Customer experience: Predict tip likelihood or trip satisfaction proxies

  Probable Prediction Targets

  Target Candidate: fare_amount / total_amount
  Problem Type: Regression
  Business Rationale: Revenue prediction, pricing strategy
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Target Candidate: trip_duration
  Problem Type: Regression
  Business Rationale: ETA optimization, driver scheduling
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Target Candidate: tip_amount > 0
  Problem Type: Classification
  Business Rationale: Incentive modeling, service quality
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Target Candidate: passenger_count
  Problem Type: Classification/Regression
  Business Rationale: Vehicle type dispatch optimization
  ────────────────────────────────────────

  Problem Type: Forecasting
  Business Rationale: Supply-demand balancing

  Critical Pre-Project Questions

  Before any analysis, must clarify with stakeholders:
  1. Primary decision: What specific action will this model enable? (e.g.,
  "reposition empty cabs 15 mins before predicted demand surge")
  2. Success metric: Is the business optimizing for revenue, customer wait time,
   driver utilization, or regulatory compliance?
  3. Data freshness: How frequently will predictions be made? (Real-time vs.
  hourly batch affects feature engineering)
  4. Constraints: Are there legal restrictions on using certain features (e.g.,
  precise GPS coordinates for privacy)?
  5. Stakeholder ownership: Who will act on predictions? (Operations team needs
  different outputs than pricing team)

  Domain Knowledge Requirements

  - Taxi economics: Understanding of fare structures, toll variations,
  time-based surcharges
  - Urban mobility patterns: Rush hour effects, event-driven demand (concerts,
  sports), weather impacts
  - Data quirks: Knowledge of common taxi dataset issues (e.g.,
  store_and_fwd_flag indicating offline trips, coordinate outliers from GPS
  drift)
  - Validation assumptions: Early verification needed for:
    - Whether trip_distance aligns with Haversine calculation from coordinates
    - If timestamps are in local time or UTC (critical for feature extraction)
    - Whether fare components sum correctly to total amount (data integrity
  check)

  2. DATASET INSPECTION STRATEGY

  Step-by-Step Inspection Protocol

  (Each step includes WHY, WHAT insights, and DECISION dependencies)

  A. Basic Structure & Shape

  - Action: df.shape, df.columns.tolist(), df.info()
  - WHY: Establishes scope and identifies immediate red flags (e.g., 500k rows
  but only 5 columns suggests aggregation)
  - WHAT insights:
    - Row count indicates statistical power for modeling
    - Column count reveals feature richness vs. curse of dimensionality risk
  - DECISIONS:
    - If <10k rows: Avoid complex models; prioritize bias-variance tradeoff
    - If >1M rows: Consider sampling for EDA; plan distributed computing

  B. Feature Typology Assessment

  - Action: Separate columns by dtype (numerical vs. object/categorical); check
  for datetime strings masquerading as objects
  - WHY: Misclassified dtypes break modeling pipelines (e.g., treating ZIP codes
   as numerical)
  - WHAT insights:
    - Identifies candidate datetime features (pickup_datetime stored as string)
    - Flags high-cardinality IDs masquerading as categorical (e.g., medallion as
   object)
  - DECISIONS:
    - Datetime columns require feature extraction (hour, day of week) NOT direct
   modeling
    - ID-like columns (trip_id, medallion) must be excluded as predictors
  (leakage risk)

  C. Numerical vs. Categorical Deep Dive

  - Action: For numerical: describe(); for categorical: value_counts().head(20),
   nunique()
  - WHY: Reveals scale mismatches and sparsity issues
  - WHAT insights:
    - Numerical: Detects impossible values (negative trip distance, 100+
  passenger count)
    - Categorical: Exposes rare categories (e.g., 1 vendor with 99.9% market
  share)
  - DECISIONS:
    - High-cardinality categoricals (>50 levels) need encoding strategy
  (target/hashed vs. one-hot)
    - Skewed numericals require transformation consideration

  D. Data Quality Scan

  - Action: isnull().sum(), duplicated().sum(), check for whitespace in strings
  - WHY: Missing/duplicate data biases estimates and inflates variance
  - WHAT insights:
    - Systematic missingness (e.g., all dropoff_coords missing for airport
  trips) indicates collection bias
    - Exact duplicates suggest ETL pipeline issues; fuzzy duplicates may
  indicate data entry errors
  - DECISIONS:
  ▎ 15% missing in a column: Consider dropping or sophisticated imputation
    - Duplicates >1%: Investigate source before deduplication

  E. Suspicious Column Investigation

  - Action: Manual inspection of columns with vague names (e.g., misc_fee, flag)
  - WHY: Undocumented features risk leakage or meaningless noise
  - WHAT insights:
    - Columns like store_and_fwd_flag require domain knowledge (indicates
  offline batch upload)
    - Derived columns (e.g., calculated_fare) may leak target information
  - DECISIONS:
    - Any column whose meaning isn't 100% clear must be validated with data
  dictionary
    - Drop features that are direct mathematical transformations of others
  (multicollinearity risk)

  F. Identifier & Constant Feature Check

  - Action: Identify columns with all unique values (trip_id) or single value
  (constant)
  - WHY: Identifiers cause overfitting; constants provide zero information
  - WHAT insights:
    - Constant features often result from failed ETL (e.g., all rate_code=1 due
  to filtering)
    - Near-constant features (>99% same value) act like constants
  - DECISIONS:
    - Drop all identifiers and constants immediately
    - For near-constants: Domain decision - if meaningful (e.g., rare payment
  type), keep; else drop

  G. Memory Usage & Storage Check

  - Action: df.memory_usage(deep=True).sum() / 1024**2
  - WHY: Prevents OOM errors during modeling; guides sampling strategy
  - WHAT insights:
    - High memory usage from string columns (e.g., long addresses) vs. efficient
   numericals
    - Object dtypes consuming space equivalent to numericals indicate
  inefficient storage
  - DECISIONS:
    - If >1GB: Plan for dtype optimization (float64→float32, category for
  strings)
    - If memory-bound: Consider out-of-core processing or feature hashing

  Critical Inspection Dependencies

  - Leakage check must precede EDA: Using future information (e.g., dropoff_time
   to predict pickup_duration) invalidates all results
  - Business context guides feature prioritization: If predicting real-time
  ETAs, drop features unavailable at prediction time (e.g., actual tip amount)
  - Initial shape determines validation strategy: Time series data requires
  temporal splits, not random

  3. DATA QUALITY ASSESSMENT

  Systematic Quality Issue Detection & Impact Analysis

  A. Missing Values

  - Detection:
    - Visual: Missingno matrix/bar charts
    - Quantitative: % missing per column, correlation of missingness between
  columns
    - Pattern: df.isnull().sum() + conditional checks (e.g., missing
  dropoff_coords only when store_and_fwd_flag=1)
  - Why dangerous:
    - Complete case analysis throws away data; biased if not MCAR (Missing
  Completely at Random)
    - ML algorithms handle missingness poorly (most require complete data)
  - Performance impact:
    - Imputation introduces variance; sophisticated methods (MICE) add
  complexity
    - Missingness in key features (e.g., trip_distance) may be more informative
  than imputed values

  B. Incorrect Datatypes

  - Detection:
    - Attempt conversions (pd.to_numeric, pd.to_datetime) and track failures
    - Check for strings in numerical columns (e.g., "N/A" in fare columns)
    - Validate datetime ranges (e.g., pickup year 1900 or 2050)
  - Why dangerous:
    - Silent failures: "123" treated as categorical vs numerical changes model
  behavior
    - Datetime errors break temporal features (e.g., extracting hour from
  invalid date)
  - Performance impact:
    - Type mismatches cause unexpected errors in vectorized operations
    - Incorrect datetime parsing destroys seasonality signal

  C. Invalid Entries & Inconsistent Formatting

  - Detection:
    - Domain-specific range checks (fare ≥0, passenger_count 1-6, coordinates
  within city bounds)
    - Categorical: value_counts() for unexpected values (e.g.,
  payment_type="Credit" vs "credit")
    - Formatting: Leading/trailing spaces, inconsistent case in strings
  - Why dangerous:
    - Invalid entries often indicate systematic errors (e.g., meter malfunction)
    - Inconsistent categoricals create artificial sparsity (e.g., 20 versions of
   "Visa")
  - Performance impact:
    - Noise increases irreducible error; models learn spurious patterns
    - Categorical inconsistency inflates feature cardinality unnecessarily

  D. Duplicate Records

  - Detection:
    - Exact duplicates: df.duplicated().sum()
    - Near-duplicates: Fuzzy matching on key columns (trip_id + pickup_time
  within 1 sec)
    - Business-key duplicates: Same trip logged by multiple systems
  - Why dangerous:
    - Over-represents frequent trips; biases empirical distributions
    - May indicate ETL pipeline replaying same data
  - Performance impact:
    - Artificially reduces variance in estimates; overconfident model
  predictions
    - Distorts frequency-based encoding (e.g., target encoding)

  E. Noise in Data

  - Detection:
    - Visual: Scatterplots of correlated variables (fare vs distance) showing
  random scatter
    - Statistical: Low signal-to-noise ratio in preliminary correlations
    - Residual analysis from ultra-simple model (e.g., mean prediction)
  - Why dangerous:
    - Masks true relationships; requires more data to detect signals
    - Often irreducible (e.g., random passenger behavior affecting tips)
  - Performance impact:
    - Increases Bayes error rate; sets upper limit on achievable model
  performance
    - Wastes model capacity fitting noise instead of signal

  F. Outliers

  - Detection:
    - Univariate: IQR method (Q1-1.5IQR, Q3+1.5IQR), Z-score (>3|), modified
  Z-score
    - Bivariate: Scatterplot residuals, Cook's distance from linear regression
    - Multivariate: Isolation Forest, Local Outlier Factor (for diagnostic only)
  - Why dangerous:
    - May represent errors (GPS drift → 100km trip in 2 mins) OR rare events
  (airport runs)
    - Skew parameter estimates in linear models; affect tree splits
  - Performance impact:
    - Outliers in targets inflate error metrics disproportionately (MSE
  sensitive to squares)
    - Can cause models to overfit to rare anomalies if not handled

  G. Imbalanced Classes & Rare Categories

  - Detection:
    - Classification: value_counts(normalize=True) for target
    - Categorical features: Frequency table; identify categories <1% prevalence
    - Rare combinations: Cross-tabulation of key categoricals (vendor × payment
  type)
  - Why dangerous:
    - Accuracy becomes meaningless; models predict majority class
    - Rare categories cause high variance in encoding (e.g., one-hot creates
  sparse columns)
  - Performance impact:
    - Biased probability estimates; poor calibration for minority class
    - Rare categorical levels may be dropped entirely in cross-validation folds

  Quality Assessment Workflow

  1. Automated screening: Run standardized checks (missing, dtype, ranges)
  2. Domain validation: Flag implausible values for SME review (e.g., "Is a $500
   fare plausible?")
  3. Pattern analysis: Check if issues cluster (e.g., all missing dropoff coords
   at night)
  4. Impact quantification: Simulate effect of removal/imputation on key
  distributions
  5. Decision tree:
    - If error is random and <5%: Simple imputation/drop
    - If systematic: Root cause fix preferred; if impossible, model-aware
  handling
    - If rare but valid: Preserve; consider specialized modeling

  4. EXPLORATORY DATA ANALYSIS (EDA) ROADMAP

  A. Univariate Analysis Strategy

  Distribution Analysis

  - Action:
    - Numerical: Histograms + KDE plots overlaid; QQ-plots vs normal
    - Categorical: Sorted bar charts (frequency descending)
  - WHY:
    - Reveals normality assumptions violations; guides transformation choice
    - Shows dominant categories for categorical features
  - INSIGHTS:
    - Fare amounts typically right-skewed (many short trips, few long hauls)
    - Passenger count often peaks at 1-2 with long tail to 6+
  - DECISIONS:
    - Severe skew (>|2|) necessitates log/sqrt transformation for linear models
    - Uniform categorical distribution suggests good feature diversity

  Skewness Analysis

  - Action: Calculate skew/kurtosis; visually assess tail weight
  - WHY:
    - Quantifies asymmetry; critical for parametric tests and linear models
    - High kurtosis indicates outlier sensitivity
  - INSIGHTS:
    - Trip distance often shows positive skew (exponential decay of long trips)
    - Fare/km ratio may be symmetric if pricing is linear
  - DECISIONS:
    - Skew >1: Consider Yeo-Johnson transformation (handles zeros/negatives)
    - High kurtosis: Robust models (Huber loss, quantile regression) may help

  Frequency Distribution

  - Action:
    - Categorical: Pareto chart (cumulative frequency)
    - Numerical: Bin sensitivity analysis (try 10, 50, 100 bins)
  - WHY:
    - Identifies long-tail categories requiring special handling
    - Reveals modality (uni/bimodal) suggesting subgroups
  - INSIGHTS:
    - Vendor distribution often shows duopoly (2 vendors 90%+ share)
    - Payment type may have cash/credit bimodality
  - DECISIONS:
    - Categories <0.1%: Group as "Other" or drop if meaningless
    - Bimodal numericals: Consider mixture models or segmentation

  Outlier Detection

  - Action:
    - Numerical: Boxplots (identify fences), scatterplot matrix for contextual
  outliers
    - Categorical: N/A (outliers don't apply; focus on rarity)
  - WHY:
    - Distinguishes global outliers (univariate) from contextual (multivariate)
    - Informs whether to winsorize, transform, or investigate
  - INSIGHTS:
    - Fare outliers often correlate with airport trips or tolls
    - Distance outliers frequently from GPS errors in urban canyons
  - DECISIONS:
    - Contextual outliers (e.g., short trip with high fare) may be valid
  (waiting time)
    - Pure measurement errors (negative distance) require correction or removal

  Feature Spread Analysis

  - Action:
    - Numerical: IQR, range, median absolute deviation (MAD)
    - Categorical: Entropy, Gini impurity
  - WHY:
    - Measures feature informativeness; low spread = low predictive power
    - Complements correlation analysis for nonlinear relationships
  - INSIGHTS:
    - Features like store_and_fwd_flag (mostly 0) have low spread
    - Temporal features (hour of day) show high spread if data spans weeks
  - DECISIONS:
    - Drop features with near-zero spread (after verifying not constant)
    - High-spread features prioritized for interaction terms

  B. Bivariate Analysis Strategy

  Numerical vs Target Analysis

  - Action:
    - Scatterplots with LOWESS smooth; conditional boxplots (bin numerical)
    - Correlation: Pearson (linear), Spearman (monotonic), Kendall (rank)
    - Categorical target: Violin plots or ridge plots
  - WHY:
    - Captures linear and nonlinear relationships missed by correlation alone
    - Reveals heteroscedasticity (changing variance)
  - INSIGHTS:
    - Fare vs distance: Usually linear but with increasing variance at long
  distances
    - Trip duration vs time of day: Strong diurnal pattern (rush hour peaks)
  - DECISIONS:
    - Nonlinear relationships: Consider splines or polynomial features
    - Heteroscedasticity: May require weighted regression or
  variance-stabilizing transform

  Categorical vs Target Analysis

  - Action:
    - Numerical target: Grouped boxplots/violin plots; ANOVA F-test
    - Categorical target: Stacked bar charts; chi-square test of independence
    - Calculate conditional means/modes and effect sizes (eta-squared, Cramer's
  V)
  - WHY:
    - Quantifies predictive power of categoricals beyond simple frequency
    - Reveals masking effects (e.g., vendor effect disappears when controlling
  for location)
  - INSIGHTS:
    - Airport trips (rate_code=2) show significantly higher fares
    - Payment type influences tip percentage (credit cards yield higher tips)
  - DECISIONS:
    - Low effect size (<0.01): Consider dropping unless theoretically important
    - Significant interactions: Preserve for modeling (e.g., vendor × time of
  day)

  Correlation Analysis & Relationship Discovery

  - Action:
    - Numerical: Correlation matrix heatmap (mask upper triangle); clustermap
    - Mixed: Correlation ratio (eta) for categorical→numerical
    - Spatial: Pickup-dropoff distance vs straight-line distance (detours)
  - WHY:
    - Identifies redundant features; detects suppressor variables
    - Reveals multicollinearity sources before VIF calculation
  - INSIGHTS:
    - Pickup/drop-off longitude highly correlated (both reflect borough)
    - Fare components often correlate strongly with total fare (by construction)
  - DECISIONS:
    - |Pearson| >0.8: Investigate for redundancy or multicollinearity
    - Low correlation but high mutual information: Suggests nonlinear
  relationship

  Trend Analysis (if temporal)

  - Action:
    - Time series plots: Daily/weekly aggregates of key metrics
    - Seasonal decomposition (STL) of trip volume/fare averages
    - Holiday/event impact analysis
  - WHY:
    - Separates trend, seasonality, and residual components
    - Identifies exogenous drivers (weather, events) for feature engineering
  - INSIGHTS:
    - Trip volume shows weekly pattern (low weekends, high weekdays)
    - Fare per mile increases during rush hour due to congestion pricing
  - DECISIONS:
    - Strong seasonality: Requires seasonal features or SARIMA baseline
    - Clear holiday effects: Binary holiday flags essential

  C. Multivariate Analysis Strategy

  Feature Interaction Analysis

  - Action:
    - Partial dependence plots (from simple RF) for 2-way interactions
    - ANOVA-style interaction plots (means by factor combinations)
    - Variance decomposition (Shapley values from interpretable model)
  - WHY:
    - Reveals non-additive effects (e.g., fare impact of distance depends on
  time)
    - Prevents missing critical synergies in feature engineering
  - INSIGHTS:
    - Long distance trips earn more per mile at night (less congestion)
    - Short trips in Manhattan have higher fare/mile than outer boroughs
  - DECISIONS:
    - Strong interactions: Create explicit features (distance × time_of_day_bin)
    - Weak interactions: Rely on model to capture (tree-based methods)

  Dependency Analysis

  - Action:
    - Conditional entropy: H(target | feature1, feature2) vs H(target |
  feature1)
    - Mutual information score for feature sets
    - Decision tree feature importance (gain/cover)
  - WHY:
    - Quantifies unique vs redundant information contribution
    - Identifies features that only matter in combination
  - INSIGHTS:
    - Pickup zone adds little if dropoff zone known (and vice versa) for fare
  prediction
    - Weather features only improve prediction during extreme events
  - DECISIONS:
    - Low conditional mutual information: Feature likely redundant
    - High synergy: Preserve both features; consider interaction term

  Cluster Patterns & Hidden Relationships

  - Action:
    - Dimensionality reduction for visualization: t-SNE/UMAP on sampled data
    - Clustering: K-means, DBSCAN on feature space (standardized)
    - Profile clusters: Compare distributions of target and features
  - WHY:
    - Discovers natural segments in data (e.g., trip types)
    - Reveals confounding variables masked in aggregate analysis
  - INSIGHTS:
    - Cluster 1: Short downtown trips (high frequency, low fare)
    - Cluster 2: Airport runs (low frequency, high fare, specific temporal
  pattern)
    - Cluster 3: Outer borough commutes (medium distance, consistent timing)
  - DECISIONS:
    - Distinct clusters: Consider stratified modeling or cluster-as-feature
    - Overlapping clusters: Global model with interaction terms sufficient

  Segment-Based Insights

  - Action:
    - Define business-relevant segments (e.g., "airport trips", "weekend
  nights")
    - Compare segment statistics to global distributions
    - Test for segment-specific model performance
  - WHY:
    - Ensures model works for economically important subgroups
    - Prevents averaging out critical behaviors (e.g., model ignores airport
  premiums)
  - INSIGHTS:
    - Weekend night trips have 30% higher fare/mile but lower trip volume
    - Electric vehicle trips (if tagged) show different tipping behavior
  - DECISIONS:
    - Poor segment performance: Segment-specific models or weighting
    - Heterogeneous effects: Interaction terms essential

  D. Business Insight Discovery Framework

  Pattern Recognition Priorities

  - Revenue drivers:
    - Which factors most increase fare per mile? (time, location, passenger
  count)
    - Are there "sweet spots" for driver earnings (trip length vs time)?
  - Operational efficiency:
    - What pickup locations yield shortest passenger wait times?
    - How does driver utilization vary by zone/time?
  - Customer behavior:
    - When are passengers most generous with tips?
    - Does trip experience (speed, smoothness) correlate with tipping?
  - Anomaly detection:
    - What characterizes trips with excessive tolls or long wait times?
    - Are certain vendor/payment combinations associated with disputes?

  Insight-to-Action Translation

  - Pricing:
    - If fare/mile varies predictably by time/zone → implement dynamic pricing
  zones
  - Repositioning:
    - If pickup demand forecasts show clear hotspots → proactive driver
  relocation
  - Driver incentives:
    - If certain trip types yield higher tips → recognize/reward those behaviors
  - Service design:
    - If airport trips have consistent pain points → dedicated queue management
  - Regulatory compliance:
    - If certain fare components frequently disputed → simplify or clarify

  EDA Influence on Product Decisions

  - Feature prioritization:
    - EDA showing pickup_hour explains 40% of fare variance → invest in
  real-time time features
  - Model complexity:
    - Strong linear trends → start with GLM before trying neural nets
  - Data collection:
    - Missing weather data correlates with forecast errors → add weather API
  - Risk mitigation:
    - EDA reveals 2% of trips have impossible speeds → add GPS validation layer

  5. VISUALIZATION STRATEGY

  Plot Selection Rationale by Data Type & Goal

  For Numerical Features

  - Histograms:
    - Why: First-line distribution check; shows modality, skew, range
    - Best for: Initial screening of all numericals; comparing pre/post
  transformation
    - Avoid when: Multimodal distributions (use mixture plots instead)
  - KDE Plots:
    - Why: Smooth density estimate; better for multimodal than histograms
    - Best for: Comparing distributions across groups (e.g., fare by payment
  type)
    - Avoid when: Small sample sizes (<100) → oversmoothing risk
  - Boxplots:
    - Why: Robust outlier detection; compares spread and central tendency
    - Best for: Categorical vs numerical (e.g., fare distribution per borough)
    - Avoid when: Sample size very large → overplotting (use violin instead)
  - Violin Plots:
    - Why: Combines boxplot + KDE; shows full distribution shape
    - Best for: When modality matters (e.g., bimodal trip durations)
    - Avoid when: Too many categories (>10) → cluttered
  - Scatterplots:
    - Why: Reveals relationships, outliers, heteroscedasticity
    - Best for: Numerical vs numerical (fare vs distance); add hue for
  categorical
    - Enhance with: LOWESS smooth, marginal histograms, transparency for
  overplotting
  - Pairplots:
    - Why: Rapid multivariate relationship scan
    - Best for: <5 features; early EDA to spot strong correlations
    - Avoid when: >6 features → overwhelming and slow

  For Categorical Features

  - Countplots:
    - Why: Shows frequency and order; baseline for categorical understanding
    - Best for: All categoricals; sorting by frequency reveals long tails
    - Enhance with: Percentage annotations; horizontal for long category lists
  - Bar Charts:
    - Why: Flexible for aggregated stats (mean fare per category)
    - Best for: Categorical vs numerical (average tip by payment type)
    - Avoid when: Too many categories → sort or group low-frequency
  - Heatmaps:
    - Why: Shows co-occurrence patterns (e.g., pickup-dropoff zone matrix)
    - Best for: Two categoricals with moderate cardinality (<20 each)
    - Avoid when: Extreme sparsity → most cells zero (use sparse matrix plot)

  For Relationships & Diagnostics

  - Correlation Heatmaps:
    - Why: Rapid multicollinearity scan; identifies feature groups
    - Best for: All numerical features; mask upper triangle for clarity
    - Enhance with: Significance stars (p<0.05) and value annotations
  - Residual Plots:
    - Why: Critical for regression model validation (patterns =
  misspecification)
    - Best for: Predicted vs residuals; scale-location plot for
  heteroscedasticity
    - Always use: After fitting any model, even baseline
  - QQ-Plots:
    - Why: Assesses normality of errors/residuals
    - Best for: Regression diagnostics; departures at tails indicate outliers
  - Learning Curves:
    - Why: Diagnoses bias/variance tradeoff; shows if more data helps
    - Best for: Mid-to-late stage modeling; training size vs validation score

  Specialized Visualizations

  - Geospatial:
    - Pickup/dropoff maps: Hexbin plots (avoid overplotting points); choropleths
   by zone
    - Flow maps: Arrow thickness = trip volume between zones (reveals corridors)
    - Why: Essential for spatial data; reveals geographic biases invisible in
  tables
  - Time-Series:
    - Decomposition plots: Observed = trend + seasonal + residual (STL)
    - Autocorrelation plots: Identifies lagged relationships
    - Why: Non-negotiable for temporal data; prevents flawed CV splits
  - Calibration Curves:
    - Why: Checks if predicted probabilities match observed frequency
  (classification)
    - Best for: Probabilistic classifiers; essential for business decisions

  Visualization Hierarchy by Insight Value

  1. Highest value:
    - Scatterplots with conditional coloring (e.g., fare ~ distance, colored by
  time_of_day)
    - Small multiples (e.g., fare distribution per hour of day)
    - Geospatial hexbin with value aggregation
  2. High value:
    - Violin plots for key categorical targets
    - Correlation heatmap with hierarchical clustering
    - Partial dependence plots from interpretable model
  3. Medium value:
    - Univariate distributions (histograms/KDE)
    - Basic countplots/bar charts
    - Time series aggregates
  4. Lower value (use sparingly):
    - Pairplots (>4 features)
    - Raw scatterplots without context
    - 3D plots (rarely add value over 2D + interaction)

  Critical Visualization Principles

  - Always annotate:
    - Sample size (n=) in subtitle
    - Key statistics (median, IQR) in caption
    - Data source and timestamp
  - Consistent encoding:
    - Same color scheme for same variable across plots (e.g., blue = vendor A)
    - Sequential palettes for ordered data; diverging for centered data
  - Avoid deception:
    - Never start numerical axes at non-zero unless justified (misleading)
    - Use perceptual uniformity in color scales (viridis, plasma)
  - Interactivity for exploration:
    - Static plots for reports; interactive (Plotly/Bokeh) for deep dives
    - Tooltips showing exact values and related fields

  6. FEATURE ENGINEERING STRATEGY

  Core Philosophy

  Engineer features that represent actionable business concepts, not just
  mathematical transformations. Every feature should be explainable to a domain
  expert in one sentence.

  Categorical Encoding Strategy

  Ordinal Encoding

  - When to use:
    - Inherent order with consistent intervals (e.g., education level: HS < BS <
   MS < PhD)
    - Time-based categories with equal spacing (hour of day 0-23)
  - Why:
    - Preserves order; minimal dimensionality increase
    - Works well with linear and tree-based models
  - Risk:
    - False assumption of equal intervals (e.g., hour 23→0 is not 1 hour)
    - Mitigation: Use only for provably ordinal; validate with domain expert

  One-Hot Encoding (OHE)

  - When to use:
    - Low cardinality (<10 levels); no natural order
    - Linear models requiring explicit category representation
  - Why:
    - No false ordinal assumption; interpretable coefficients
    - Standard expectation in many ML libraries
  - Risk:
    - Dimensionality explosion with high cardinality
    - Sparsity issues; multicollinearity if drop-first not used
  - Mitigation:
    - Drop first level to avoid dummy variable trap
    - Group rare categories before OHE (see below)

  Target/Mean Encoding

  - When to use:
    - High cardinality categoricals (>50 levels)
    - When target has monotonic relationship with category
    - Tree-based models less affected by cardinality
  - Why:
    - Captures target relationship efficiently; single numerical feature
    - Reduces sparsity vs OHE
  - Risk:
    - Target leakage (using future/test data to compute means)
    - Overfitting to rare categories (high variance)
  - Mitigation:
    - Critical: Compute encoding statistics only on training fold
    - Use smoothing: encoded = (category_count * category_mean + global_mean * 
  smooth) / (category_count + smooth)
    - Validate with cross-tabulation: check for monotonic relationship

  Frequency/Count Encoding

  - When to use:
    - When category frequency predicts target (e.g., popular pickup zones)
    - As proxy for target encoding when leakage concerns exist
  - Why:
    - No target leakage; captures popularity signal
    - Stable for rare categories (frequency never zero)
  - Risk:
    - Weak predictive power if frequency not related to target
    - Loses category-specific information
  - Mitigation:
    - Validate with ANOVA: does frequency explain target variance?
    - Combine with target encoding for best of both worlds

  Embedding Techniques

  - When to use:
    - Very high cardinality (>100 levels) with sufficient data
    - When relationships are complex and nonlinear
    - Deep learning or factorization machine contexts
  - Why:
    - Learns dense, low-dimensional representation
    - Captures similarity (e.g., geographically close zones have similar
  embeddings)
  - Risk:
    - Black box; harder to interpret
    - Requires more data to train effectively
  - Mitigation:
    - Start with simple encoding; only use embeddings if baseline underfits
    - Visualize embeddings to verify spatial/temporal coherence

  Handling Rare Categories

  - Detection:
    - Categories with <0.1% frequency OR <20 samples (adjust for dataset size)
    - Cross-validate: Does category appear in all CV folds?
  - Strategies:
    - Group as "Other": Simple; preserves signal if rare categories behave
  similarly
    - Drop entirely: Only if meaningless noise (e.g., typos); verify with domain
   expert
    - Target encoding with strong smoothing: Borrow strength from global mean
    - Hierarchical grouping: E.g., rare vendor → "other vendor"; rare zone →
  borough level
    - Predict rare category first: Model P(category) then use as feature (avoids
   leakage)
  - When NOT to engineer:
    - If rare category represents valid but uncommon business case (e.g., luxury
   taxi service)
    - If removing it creates systematic bias (e.g., dropping all airport trips)
    - If sample size is huge (>1M) → rare categories still have sufficient
  samples

  Feature Scaling Strategy

  Normalization (Min-Max)

  - When to use:
    - Algorithms sensitive to feature scale (SVM, k-NN, neural nets)
    - When bounded interpretation needed ([0,1] range)
    - Features have hard min/max (e.g., probabilities)
  - Why:
    - Preserves distribution shape; sensitive to outliers
    - Output range predictable
  - Risk:
    - Outliers compress majority of data into small range
    - Not robust to new outliers outside training min/max

  Standardization (Z-Score)

  - When to use:
    - Algorithms assuming normality (linear regression, LDA, logistic
  regression)
    - PCA, clustering (k-means, Gaussian mixture)
    - When outliers present but not extreme
  - Why:
    - Robust to linear transformations; preserves distribution shape
    - Outliers affect scale less than normalization
  - Risk:
    - Assumes roughly symmetric distribution; poor for skewed data
    - Mean/std sensitive to outliers (use robust scaler if severe)

  Robust Scaling

  - When to use:
    - Presence of outliers; non-normal distributions
    - Algorithms using median/IQR (random forests less sensitive but still
  benefit)
  - Why:
    - Uses IQR and median; ignores outliers beyond fences
    - Better for skewed data (e.g., fare amounts)
  - Risk:
    - Less common; may confuse stakeholders unfamiliar with IQR scaling
    - Output range not bounded (can be negative)

  Specialized Transformations

  - Log Transform:
    - When: Right-skewed positives (fare, distance); multiplicative effects
    - Why: Stabilizes variance; makes relationships more linear
    - Caveat: Requires positive values; use log(x+1) for zeros
  - Box-Cox / Yeo-Johnson:
    - When: Need to normalize distribution; parametric test prep
    - Why: Finds optimal power transform; Yeo-Johnson handles negatives/zeros
    - Caveat: Transformed units hard to interpret; must invert for reporting
  - Quantile Transform:
    - When: Severe non-normality; want uniform or normal distribution
    - Why: Non-parametric; preserves rank order
    - Risk: Distorts relationships; use only as last resort

  Interaction Features

  - Creation Principles:
    - Domain-driven: Only create interactions with clear business meaning (e.g.,
   distance × congestion_factor)
    - Statistical guidance:
        - High mutual information between features suggests interaction
  potential
      - Low partial dependence plot additivity
      - Significant interaction term in linear model
    - Limit order:
        - Start with 2-way interactions; 3-way+ rare and hard to interpret
      - Prioritize interactions involving target-correlated features
  - Encoding:
    - Numerical × Numerical: Simple product (consider log-transformed inputs)
    - Numerical × Categorical:
        - Create separate slopes per category (equivalent to interaction)
      - Or: Numerical feature × category indicator (one-hot)
    - Categorical × Categorical:
        - Only if both low cardinality; otherwise use target encoding of
  combination
      - Or: Create interaction term via cross-validation to avoid leakage
  - When NOT to create:
    - If features are already combined in target (e.g., fare = base + rate × 
  distance)
    - If interaction lacks business logic (e.g., vendor_id × payment_type
  without mechanistic reason)
    - If causes severe multicollinearity (check VIF after creation)

  Aggregated Features

  - Temporal Aggregations:
    - Rolling statistics:
        - Mean fare in last 1hr at pickup zone (captures immediate demand)
      - Standard deviation of trip duration in last 24h (reliability measure)
    - Expanding window:
        - Cumulative average tip rate by driver (experience proxy)
      - Cumulative trips served by vendor today (market share)
  - Spatial Aggregations:
    - Zone-based:
        - Average pickup/dropoff frequency per zone (popularity)
      - Typical fare matrix between zones (profitability surface)
    - Distance-based:
        - Number of trips within 1km radius in last 30m (local density)
      - Average speed in pickup zone (congestion proxy)
  - Creation Protocol:
    - Critical: Ensure no lookahead bias
        - For time t, only use data from < t
      - Use expanding window for cumulative stats; sliding window for recent
    - Validation:
        - Check aggregation stability (does 1hr mean change drastically with
  window?)
      - Compare to business KPIs (e.g., does zone popularity match dispatch
  logs?)
  - Pitfalls to Avoid:
    - Using future data (e.g., "average fare of trips ending after pickup")
    - Overly specific windows (e.g., exact 47-minute window) without
  justification
    - Ignoring edge effects (first/last hours of day have incomplete windows)

  Date/Time Feature Extraction

  - Essential Components:
    - Cyclical encoding:
        - Hour of day: sin(2π*hour/24), cos(2π*hour/24) (preserves 23→0
  continuity)
      - Day of week: Similar with period=7
      - Day of month: Period=30.44 (average month length)
      - Month of year: Period=12
    - Business-relevant binning:
        - Rush hour: 7-9 AM, 4-7 PM (binary or intensity)
      - Weekend: Friday 6PM - Monday 6AM (accounts for cultural variation)
      - Holiday: Federal/local holiday flags + eve/day after
      - Shift: Day (7AM-3PM), Evening (3PM-11PM), Night (11PM-7AM)
    - Timestamp math:
        - Time since last trip (driver idle time)
      - Time until next scheduled event (if known)
      - Duration of current shift (if logs available)
  - Why Cyclical > Integer:
    - Prevents model from thinking hour 23 is "far" from hour 0
    - Captures periodic nature essential for temporal patterns
  - When to Avoid:
    - If data spans <1 week → day of week meaningless
    - If prediction horizon >> temporal pattern (e.g., forecasting yearly
  annuals from hourly data)
    - If timestamp is corrupted or missing systematically

  Binning & Discretization

  - When to use:
    - Strong nonlinear relationship with target (e.g., fare/mile peaks at medium
   distances)
    - Creating interpretable risk categories (e.g., "high congestion" zone)
    - Preparing for monotonic constraints in modeling
  - Methods:
    - Equal-width: Simple; sensitive to outliers
    - Equal-frequency (quantile): Robust to outliers; ensures bin population
    - Business-defined: Clinically meaningful thresholds (e.g., speed <10mph =
  congested)
    - Model-based: Optimal bins from decision tree splits (use caution → leakage
   risk)
  - Why prefer equal-frequency:
    - Handles skewed distributions naturally
    - Prevents empty bins in sparse regions
  - Risk:
    - Loses within-bin information; assumes homogeneity
    - Number of bins hyperparameter requiring validation
  - Mitigation:
    - Validate with ANOVA: does binning significantly explain target?
    - Start with 3-5 bins; increase only if residual plots show pattern
    - Consider smooth alternatives (splines) if binning feels arbitrary

  Domain-Driven Features (Taxi-Specific Examples)

  - Geospatial:
    - Haversine distance (more accurate than Euclidean for lat/lon)
    - Manhattan distance (better proxy for grid-based city travel)
    - Route directness: Haversine / actual route length (from map matching)
    - Pickup/dropoff in specific zones: Airport, business district,
  entertainment area
  - Temporal:
    - Time of day in seconds since midnight (for cyclical encoding)
    - Minutes to/from nearest rush hour boundary
    - Is pickup during scheduled public transit strike? (if data available)
  - Financial:
    - Fare per mile (efficiency metric; detects meter tampering)
    - Tip percentage (service quality proxy)
    - Proportion of fare from tolls/surcharges (route characteristic)
  - Operational:
    - Vehicle utilization: Trip time / (trip time + idle time)
    - Detour index: Actual distance / straight-line distance
    - Load factor: Passenger count / vehicle capacity
  - Validation:
    - Each feature must pass "so what?" test:
        - "If this feature doubles, what business action would we take?"
      - If no clear answer, question its utility

  Feature Engineering Guardrails

  - Never engineer using target:
    - Absolute prohibition on features incorporating
  future/prediction-time-unknown data
    - Example: Don't use actual_tip_amount to predict will_tip (use only
  pre-trip known data)
  - Validate stability:
    - Check feature distribution similarity between train/test (KS test, PSI)
    - Ensure feature meaning doesn't shift over time (e.g., "rush hour" changes
  with seasons)
  - Document assumptions:
    - Every engineered feature needs:
        - Business rationale
      - Calculation method
      - Known limitations
      - Sensitivity to input errors
  - Start minimal:
    - Begin with 5-7 core features; add complexity only if baseline underfits
    - Most gains come from cleaning and basic transforms, not exotic features

  7. FEATURE SELECTION STRATEGY

  Goal

  Identify the smallest feature subset that preserves predictive power while
  reducing overfitting risk, computational cost, and improving interpretability.

  Correlation-Based Selection

  - Method:
    - Remove one feature from pairs with |correlation| > threshold (0.7-0.9)
    - Prefer dropping the feature with:
        - Lower univariate correlation with target
      - Higher missingness
      - Less business interpretability
  - Why:
    - Rapidly reduces multicollinearity; improves numerical stability
    - Cuts redundant information that adds noise
  - Limitations:
    - Only catches linear relationships; misses nonlinear dependencies
    - Arbitrary threshold; may remove complementary features
  - Best practice:
    - Use as first pass before more sophisticated methods
    - Always verify with VIF after selection (target VIF <5-10)

  Variance Threshold Methods

  - Method:
    - Remove features with variance below threshold (e.g., <0.01 after
  standardization)
    - For binary features: Remove if prevalence <1% or >99%
  - Why:
    - Features near-constant provide negligible information
    - Reduces noise from near-duplicate or constant columns
  - Limitations:
    - Ignores relationship with target; may remove predictive low-variance
  features
    - Variance threshold scale-dependent (must standardize first)
  - Best practice:
    - Combine with univariate target correlation:
        - Keep if |corr(target, feature)| > 0.05 AND variance > threshold
    - Validate with permutation importance: does removal hurt performance?

  Statistical Testing

  - Numerical target:
    - ANOVA F-test:
        - Compares group means across categorical feature levels
      - Select categories where p-value <0.05 (with multiple testing correction)
    - Mutual information (MIC):
        - Measures general dependence; captures nonlinear
      - Use sklearn.feature_selection.mutual_info_regression
  - Categorical target:
    - Chi-square test:
        - Tests independence between feature and target
      - Requires adequate expected frequencies (>5 per cell)
    - Tschuprow's T / Cramer's V:
        - Effect size for chi-square; values >0.1 often meaningful
  - Why:
    - Provides statistical significance; controls for false discoveries
    - Mutual information captures complex relationships
  - Limitations:
    - Assumptions (normality, independence) often violated
    - Multiple testing burden; p-values sensitive to sample size
  - Best practice:
    - Focus on effect size over p-value (especially with large n)
    - Use Benjamini-Hochberg correction for FDR control
    - For MIC: k-nearest neighbors parameter requires tuning

  Feature Importance Methods

  - Tree-based importance:
    - Mean decrease impurity (MDI):
        - Fast but biased toward high-cardinality features
      - Use with caution; prefer permutation importance
    - Permutation importance:
        - Gold standard; measures performance drop when feature shuffled
      - Computationally expensive but reliable
      - Critical: Must compute on validation set, not training
  - Linear model coefficients:
    - Standardized coefficients:
        - Comparable scale; assumes linear relationship
      - Only valid if features are standardized and no multicollinearity
    - Lasso path:
        - Features with nonzero coefficients at optimal lambda
      - Naturally performs selection via L1 regularization
  - Why:
    - Captures model-specific utility; accounts for interactions
    - Permutation importance is model-agnostic and intuitive
  - Limitations:
    - MDI biased; permutation importance slow for large datasets
    - Linear coefficients meaningless if assumptions violated
  - Best practice:
    - Use permutation importance from simple baseline (e.g., logistic
  regression)
    - Validate stability: run multiple times with different seeds
    - Never use training-set importance for selection (guarantees overfitting)

  Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE)

  - Method:
        i. Train model on all features
        ii. Remove least important feature (per method above)
        iii. Repeat until k features remain or performance drops
    - Use cross-validation to select optimal k
  - Why:
    - Accounts for feature interactions in selection process
    - Wrapper method often finds better subsets than filter methods
  - Limitations:
    - Computationally expensive (O(n_features) model fits)
    - Risk of overfitting to selection criterion
    - Instability: small data changes → different selected features
  - Best practice:
    - Use with simple, stable models (e.g., linear SVM, not deep nets)
    - Always nest within cross-validation:
        - Outer loop: evaluate selected feature set
      - Inner loop: perform RFE to choose features
    - Start with large k (e.g., 50 features) and stop when performance plateaus

  Multicollinearity Detection

  - Variance Inflation Factor (VIF):
    - Calculation: VIF_j = 1 / (1 - R²_j) where R²_j is from regressing feature
  j on all others
    - Interpretation:
        - VIF=1: No multicollinearity
      - VIF>5: Moderate concern
      - VIF>10: Severe multicollinearity (consider removal)
  - Condition Number:
    - Ratio of largest to smallest eigenvalue of X'X
  ▎ 30 suggests multicollinearity issues
  - Why:
    - Multicollinearity inflates variance of coefficient estimates
    - Makes coefficients unstable and hard to interpret
    - Doesn't affect prediction accuracy but harms inferential goals
  - When to act:
    - If goal is inference (understanding drivers): Aggressive removal (VIF>5)
    - If goal is prediction only: Tolerate higher VIF (<20) if performance
  stable
  - Best practice:
    - Check VIF after encoding and scaling
    - Remove highest VIF feature iteratively until all < threshold
    - Verify removal doesn't hurt validation performance

  PCA and Dimensionality Reduction

  - When to use:
    - Features are highly correlated; latent constructs suspected
    - Need for visualization or noise reduction
    - Computational constraints with many features
  - Why:
    - Creates orthogonal components capturing max variance
    - Can denoise by keeping only top PCs (if signal in first few)
  - Limitations:
    - Components are linear combinations; hard to interpret
    - Assumes linear relationships; misses nonlinear structure
    - Signal may reside in later PCs (e.g., contrast effects)
  - Best practice:
    - Always standardize before PCA
    - Examine scree plot: look for elbow (diminishing returns)
    - Validate: Does reconstruction error vs. components show clear cutoff?
    - For interpretation: Examine loadings; group features with high |loading|
  on same PC
    - Never use PCA components directly in final model without verifying they
  improve performance over raw features

  Avoiding Overfitting During Selection

  - The Golden Rule: Feature selection must occur INSIDE the cross-validation 
  loop
    - ❌ Wrong: Select features on full data → then CV on selected features
    - ✅ Right: For each CV fold:
        i. Select features using ONLY training fold data
      ii. Train model on selected features
      iii. Evaluate on hold-out fold
    - Otherwise: Selection bias inflates performance estimates
  - Stability validation:
    - Run selection multiple times with different seeds/subsets
    - Report feature selection frequency (e.g., "hour_of_day selected in 95% of
  runs")
    - Unstable selection → rely on regularization or domain knowledge instead
  - Performance plateau check:
    - Plot CV score vs. number of features selected
    - Stop when adding features yields <0.5% improvement
    - Simpler model preferred at parity (Occam's razor)
  - Domain knowledge anchoring:
    - Force inclusion of known important features (e.g., trip distance for fare
  prediction)
    - Never select a set that contradicts strong causal knowledge
  - Holdout set purity:
    - Never touch test set until final model evaluation
    - Use validation set for all selection/tuning decisions

  Selection Workflow Summary

  1. Filter phase:
    - Remove constants, near-constants, obvious duplicates
    - Apply variance threshold + univariate correlation screen
  2. Wrapper phase:
    - Apply RFE with simple linear model (logistic/lasso)
    - Validate stability via bootstrap
  3. Embedded phase:
    - Use final model with inherent selection (lasso, tree-based)
    - Compare to filter/wrapper results
  4. Domain validation:
    - SME review: Does selected set make business sense?
    - Add back any theoretically critical features missed
  5. Final verification:
    - Train full model on selected features
    - Confirm performance matches CV estimate

  8. MACHINE LEARNING STRATEGY

  Train-Validation-Test Split Strategy

  Temporal Considerations (Critical for Taxi Data)

  - If data has temporal ordering (almost always true for trip data):
    - Never use random shuffle → causes leakage from future to past
    - Preferred: Time-based split
        - Train: Oldest 70% (e.g., Jan-Jul)
      - Validation: Next 15% (Aug)
      - Test: Most recent 15% (Sep-Oct)
    - Why:
        - Mimics real-world deployment (train on past, predict future)
      - Captures concept drift; validates model generalizes forward in time
    - Validation:
        - Check for seasonality: Ensure each split contains full weekly cycles
      - If data <3 months: Consider stratified temporal sampling by week/day
  - If purely cross-sectional (rare for trip data):
    - Stratified split by key categorical (e.g., vendor, zone) to preserve
  distribution
    - Minimum 70/15/15 split; adjust based on size
  - Holdout set size:
    - Test set: Large enough for stable metric estimation (min 1000 samples)
    - Validation set: Sufficient for tuning (min 500 samples)
    - Training set: Remainder (should be >> validation/test)

  Cross-Validation Strategy

  For Temporal Data

  - Time Series Cross-Validation (TSCV):
    - Expanding window:
        - Fold 1: Train [0:t1], Val [t1:t2]
      - Fold 2: Train [0:t2], Val [t2:t3]
      - Fold 3: Train [0:t3], Val [t3:t4]
    - Or sliding window (fixed training window size)
    - Why:
        - Respects temporal ordering; no future leakage
      - Estimates how model performance degrades over time
  - When to use:
    - Primary validation strategy for forecasting/dynamic prediction
    - Essential if detecting concept drift (e.g., changing driver behavior)
  For Non-Temporal or Augmented Temporal

  - Stratified K-Fold:
    - Stratify by target distribution (for classification) or key bins (for
  regression)
    - Ensures each fold represents overall distribution
    - Why:
        - Controls for class/label imbalance in small datasets
      - Reduces variance of performance estimate
  - Group K-Fold:
    - Group by entity (e.g., driver ID, vehicle ID)
    - Ensures all trips from same entity in same fold
    - Why:
        - Prevents overoptimistic estimates from correlated samples
      - Critical if features include driver/vehicle history
  - Avoid:
    - Standard K-Fold on temporal data → severe leakage
    - Leave-one-out → computationally prohibitive; high variance

  Baseline Model Creation

  Regression Baselines (e.g., fare_amount prediction)

  1. Naive Baseline:
    - Predict median/mode of training target
    - Why: Establishes floor; any model must beat this
  2. Linear Baseline:
    - Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) with:
        - Core features: trip_distance, pickup_hour_bin, passenger_count
      - Essential interactions: distance × time_of_day (congestion effect)
    - Why:
        - Interpretable; highlights variable importance
      - Fast to train; good for feature sanity checks
  3. Tree-Based Baseline:
    - Decision Tree Regressor (depth=3-5)
    - Why:
        - Captures nonlinearities and interactions automatically
      - Reveals important splits for feature engineering
  Classification Baselines (e.g., will_tip>0)

  1. Naive Baseline:
    - Predict training set majority class
  2. Logistic Regression Baseline:
    - With L2 regularization (to handle multicollinearity)
    - Features: Same as regression baseline + categorical encodings
  3. Decision Tree Classifier:
    - Same rationale as regression baseline

  Why These Baselines?

  - Interpretability first: Understand what drives predictions before complex
  models
  - Speed: Enables rapid iteration on features/engineering
  - Benchmarks:
    - If complex model doesn't beat linear baseline → feature issue
    - If tree baseline beats linear → strong nonlinearities/interactions
    - If neither beats naive → data quality or problem mismatch

  Candidate Algorithms to Try

  For Regression Tasks

  Algorithm: Linear Models (Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet)
  When to Use: Interpretable needs; baseline; low feature collinearity
  Strengths: Fast; interpretable coefficients; built-in feature selection
  (Lasso)
  Weaknesses: Assumes linearity; sensitive to outliers
  Taxi-Specific Notes: Good for fare/mile modeling; Lasso selects relevant
    distance/time interactions
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Algorithm: Random Forest Regressor
  When to Use: Nonlinear relationships; mixed feature types; robustness needed
  Strengths: Handles nonlinearity; feature importance; resistant to outliers
  Weaknesses: Less extrapolative; can overfit with noisy trees
  Taxi-Specific Notes: Excellent first try; captures complex zone/time
    interactions
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Algorithm: Gradient Boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost)
  When to Use: High performance needed; structured/tabular data
  Strengths: State-of-the-art for tabular; handles categoricals natively
    (CatBoost); robust
  Weaknesses: More hyperparameters; longer training
  Taxi-Specific Notes: CatBoost excels with high-cardinality zones; monitor for
    overfitting on rare trips
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Algorithm: SVR (RBF kernel)
  When to Use: Small-medium datasets; clear margin of separation
  Strengths: Effective in high dimensions; robust via epsilon-insensitive loss
  Weaknesses: Poor scalability (O(n²)); sensitive to scaling
  Taxi-Specific Notes: Rarely best for taxi data; consider only if <10k samples
    and strong theory
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Algorithm: Neural Networks
  When to Use: Very large datasets (>100k); complex spatiotemporal patterns
  Strengths: Flexible function approximator; can learn embeddings
  Weaknesses: Black box; data-hungry; tuning complex
  Taxi-Specific Notes: Only consider after GBT fails; use for spatial feature
    learning (e.g., zone embeddings)

  For Classification Tasks

  Algorithm: Logistic Regression
  When to Use: Baseline; interpretable probability needs
  Strengths: Probabilistic outputs; fast; regularization options
  Weaknesses: Linear decision boundaries
  Taxi-Specific Notes: Good for tip probability; coefficients show driver/zone
    effects
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Algorithm: Random Forest Classifier
  When to Use: Mixed data types; interaction capture
  Strengths: Handles categoricals; feature importance; robust
  Weaknesses: Probability calibration often poor
  Taxi-Specific Notes: Strong for vendor/type prediction; use calibration if
    probs needed
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Algorithm: Gradient Boosting Classifiers
  When to Use: High AUC needed; imbalanced classes
  Strengths: Excellent performance; handles class weights; sparsity
  Weaknesses: Overfitting risk; complex tuning
  Taxi-Specific Notes: XGBoost with scale_pos_weight for rare events (e.g.,
    complaints)
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Algorithm: Naive Bayes
  When to Use: Very high dimensional sparse data; strong independence assumption
  Strengths: Extremely fast; works with small n; handles missing data
  Weaknesses: Poor if features correlated
  Taxi-Specific Notes: Only consider for text-like features (unlikely in taxi
    data)
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Algorithm: Neural Networks
  When to Use: Large n; complex pattern recognition
  Strengths: Learns complex interactions; scalable with data
  Weaknesses: Requires extensive tuning; interpretability challenge
  Taxi-Specific Notes: Consider for spatiotemporal prediction (e.g., next trip
    destination)

  Ensemble Methods Strategy

  When to Employ

  - After establishing strong single-model baseline (e.g., GBT AUC=0.82)
  - When:
    - Validation shows high variance (model unstable across folds)
    - Business requires uncertainty estimates
    - Competition-level performance needed (rare for internal projects)
  Techniques

  - Bagging:
    - Random Forest is already bagged trees; further bagging RF adds little
    - Use when: High variance in single trees; need extra robustness
  - Boosting:
    - Already using if XGBoost/LightGBM/CatBoost selected
    - Avoid stacking same-type models (e.g., multiple GBTs) → diminishing
  returns
  - Stacking:
    - Base layer: Diverse models (e.g., LR, RF, GBT, NN)
    - Meta-learner: Simple model (e.g., logistic regression)
    - When to use:
        - Base models make different types of errors
      - Sufficient data (>50k) to train meta-learner without overfit
      - Business needs calibrated probabilities
    - Critical:
        - Meta-learner trained only on out-of-fold predictions from base models
      - Never use same data for base training and meta-training (leakage)
  Weighting & Blending

  - Simple average:
    - Effective if models are uncorrelated in error
    - Weights = 1/n (equal) or validation performance-based
  - Weighted average:
    - Weights inversely proportional to validation error
    - Requires error estimates from same holdout set
  - When to avoid:
    - If one model dominates (weighting adds complexity for little gain)
    - If interpretability required (ensembles harder to explain)

  Hyperparameter Tuning Strategy

  Search Space Definition

  - Start wide, then narrow:
    - Initial broad search (e.g., max_depth: 3-15, learning_rate: 0.01-0.3)
    - Narrow based on promising regions (e.g., if lr=0.1 best, search 0.05-0.2)
  - Parameter types:
    - Continuous: Learning rate, regularization strength (log scale)
    - Discrete: Tree depth, number of estimators (integer scale)
    - Categorical: Loss function, booster type
  - Dependencies:
    - e.g., max_depth only meaningful if boosting algorithm used
    - e.g., subsample and colsample_bytree interact

  Search Methods

  - Random Search:
    - Why: More efficient than grid for high-dimensional spaces; finds good
  regions faster
    - When: Early exploration; >5 hyperparameters
    - Tip: Sample from distributions (log-uniform for rates, uniform for depths)
  - Bayesian Optimization:
    - Why: Builds surrogate model; focuses search on promising areas
    - When: Moderate budget (50-100 trials); smooth response surface expected
    - Tools: scikit-optimize, Hyperopt, Optuna
  - Grid Search:
    - Why: Exhaustive; only for <3 parameters or final validation
    - When: Verifying known good region; small discrete spaces (e.g., 3x3x3)
  - Avoid:
    - Exhaustive grid over >4 dimensions (combinatorial explosion)
    - Manual tuning without tracking (wastes effort; no reproducibility)

  Validation Protocol for Tuning

  - Inner-Outer CV Loop:
    - Outer loop: Estimates generalization of tuning process (critical!)
    - Inner loop: Performs actual hyperparameter search on training folds
    - Why: Prevents tuning bias; honest estimate of tuned model performance
    - Alternative: Single train/val/test with val used for tuning (acceptable if
   large val set)
  - Early Stopping:
    - Mandatory for boosting/neural nets
    - Monitor validation loss; stop when no improvement for N rounds
    - Saves computation; reduces overfitting
  - Stability Check:
    - Run top 3 configurations 5x with different seeds
    - Report mean±std; if std large, simpler hyperparameters preferred

  Experiment Tracking Strategy

  What to Log Religiously

  - Data version:
    - Hash of training data (or snapshot ID)
    - Feature engineering script version
  - Preprocessing:
    - All scalers/encoders fitted (with parameters)
    - Train/val/test split definition (dates or indices)
  - Model:
    - Algorithm + hyperparameters (full config)
    - Random seed (for reproducibility)
    - Training time/resources used
  - Metrics:
    - Training/validation/test loss per epoch/round
    - Final metrics: RMSE, MAE, R² (regression); AUC, accuracy, F1
  (classification)
    - Calibration metrics (if probabilistic outputs needed)
  - Artifacts:
    - Model pickle/joblib file
    - Feature importance plot
    - Validation predictions (for error analysis)
  - Context:
    - Git commit hash of code
    - Notes on data issues encountered that day
    - Hypothesis being tested

  Tools & Practices

  - Lightweight:
    - CSV/JSON log + model artifacts in dated directories
    - Tools: mlflow (lightweight use), sacred, or custom JSON logger
  - Enterprise:
    - MLflow tracking server, Weights & Biases, or Neptune.ai
    - Enables comparison, model registry, collaboration
  - Critical Practices:
    - Always log failures: Negative results prevent redundant work
    - Tag experiments: baseline, feature_set_v2, tuning_run_3
    - Link to tickets: Connect experiments to Jira/Asana items for traceability
    - Archive everything: Never delete old models; store in versioned bucket

  Algorithm Selection Heuristics

  Start With:

  - Regression:
    - Random Forest Regressor (n_estimators=100, max_depth=10)
    - Why: Strong baseline; handles mixed data; feature importance for insights
  - Classification:
    - Random Forest Classifier or Logistic Regression (with
  class_weight='balanced')
    - Why: RF handles interactions; LR provides probabilities; both robust

  Progress To If Needed:

  - If baseline underfits (high train AND test error):
    - Increase model complexity:
        - GBT (XGBoost/LightGBM) with higher depth/interaction depth
      - Add polynomial/spline features
      - Consider neural net for complex patterns
  - If baseline overfits (low train, high test error):
    - Increase regularization:
        - Higher lambda (Lasso/Ridge)
      - Lower tree depth / higher min_samples_leaf
      - More dropout (if using NN)
      - Early stopping patience
  - If specific weakness:
    - Poor tail prediction (regression):
        - Quantile regression or GBM with custom loss
    - Poor rare class recall (classification):
        - Focal loss, class weighting, or threshold tuning
    - Need interpretable probabilities:
        - Logistic regression with calibration; or isotonic regression on GBT
  outputs

  Final Selection Criteria

  1. Meets business metric threshold (e.g., RMSE < $1.50 for fare prediction)
  2. Stable performance across temporal validation folds
  3. Interpretability sufficient for stakeholder trust
  4. Training/inference latency meets operational constraints
  5. Simplest model satisfying above (Occam's razor razor)

  9. EVALUATION STRATEGY

  Metric Selection Framework

  Regression Metrics

  - Primary:
    - RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error):
        - Penalizes large errors; same units as target
      - Why: Business often cares about large prediction errors (e.g., $10 fare
  error worse than two $5 errors)
    - MAE (Mean Absolute Error):
        - More robust to outliers; intuitive interpretation
      - Why: Complements RMSE; shows typical error magnitude
    - R² (Coefficient of Determination):
        - Proportion of variance explained; [0,1] range
      - Why: Quick benchmark vs. naive model; but misleading if model biased
  - Supplementary:
    - MAPE (Mean Absolute Percentage Error):
        - Relative error; useful for comparing across scales
      - Why: Business thinks in % errors ("our predictions are within 15%")
      - Caution: Infinite if actual=0; skewed by small denominators
    - Median AE:
        - Robust to outliers; less influenced by extreme errors
      - Why: Better represents "typical" user experience
  - When to prioritize:
    - RMSE: When large errors are disproportionately costly (e.g., mispricing
  long trips)
    - MAE: When all errors cost similarly per unit (e.g., driver penalty per
  minute ETA error)
    - R²: Only for communication; never as sole optimization target

  Classification Metrics

  - Primary (depends on business cost):
    - AUC-ROC:
        - Threshold-invariant; measures ranking quality
      - Why: Good for comparing models; insensitive to class imbalance
      - Limitation: Optimizes for ranking, not absolute probability accuracy
    - F1-Score:
        - Harmonic mean of precision/recall; balances FP/FN costs
      - Why: When both false positives and false negatives matter
      - Caution: Arbitrary 0.5 threshold; depends on class balance
  - Supplementary:
    - Precision-Recall AUC (PR-AUC):
        - Better than ROC-AUC when positive class rare (<10%)
      - Why: Focuses on performance where it matters (rare event detection)
    - Confusion Matrix:
        - Breaks down error types; essential for cost analysis
      - Why: Reveals if model is biased toward over/under-prediction
    - Calibration Metrics:
        - Brier score, calibration plot, Hosmer-Lemeshow test
      - Why: Critical if using probabilities for decision thresholds (e.g., "tip
   if P>0.3")
  - When to prioritize:
    - AUC-ROC: When model used for ranking (e.g., "show top 10% likely to tip")
    - F1/PR-AUC: When optimizing for discrete action with cost imbalance
    - Precision: When false positives very costly (e.g., flagging legitimate
  trips as fraud)
    - Recall: When false negatives very costly (e.g., missing safety incidents)

  ROC-AUC Strategy Deep Dive

  When to Use

  - Appropriate:
    - Binary classification with meaningful ranking
    - Comparing model discriminative ability irrespective of threshold
    - Initial model screening stage
  - Inappropriate:
    - When operating point is fixed by business rules (e.g., "alert if P>0.9")
    - When class imbalance extreme and business cares about absolute counts
    - When miscalibration risks decisions (e.g., predicted 70% chance but actual
   30%)

  Interpretation & Limitations

  - What it measures:
    - Probability that a random positive instance ranks higher than random
  negative one
    - Equivalent to Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney U statistic
  - Key insight:
    - AUC=0.5: Random classifier; AUC=1.0: Perfect ranking
    - But: Two models with same AUC can have vastly different precision at
  operating point
  - Danger zones:
    - High AUC but poor precision at needed recall (e.g., AUC=0.85 but at 80%
  recall, precision=20%)
    - AUC insensitive to class distribution; misleading if train/test prevalence
   differs
  - Best practices:
    - Always pair with precision-recall curve when prevalence <15%
    - Report AUC at specific recall levels (e.g., "AUC at 80% recall = 0.62")
    - Use partial AUC if only relevant FPR range (e.g., FPR<0.1 for screening
  tests)

  Precision-Recall Tradeoff Analysis

  Business Context Mapping

  - High precision critical when:
    - False positives trigger costly actions (e.g., refusing service based on
  fraud prediction)
    - Example: "Only flag trip as fraud if >95% certain" → optimize for
  precision at high threshold
  - High recall critical when:
    - False negatives miss important events (e.g., failing to detect unsafe
  driver)
    - Example: "Catch 95% of drunk driving incidents" → optimize for recall
  - F1-optimal when:
    - Equal cost of FP and FN; or when seeking balanced operating point
    - Example: "Maximize correct tip predictions" → F1 at threshold where
  precision=recall

  Analysis Protocol

  1. Plot precision-recall curve from validation predictions
  2. Identify business operating point:
    - Map to cost matrix:
        - Cost(FP) × FP + Cost(FN) × FN = Total expected cost
    - Find threshold minimizing expected cost
  3. Calculate metrics at operating point:
    - Precision, recall, F1, specificity
    - Expected cost per prediction
  4. Sensitivity analysis:
    - How much does cost change if threshold shifts ±0.05?
    - If flat region: robust to threshold choice; if steep: requires precise
  calibration

  Error Analysis Strategy

  Regression Error Analysis

  - Residual plots:
    - Predicted vs residuals:
        - Funnel shape → heteroscedasticity (variance changes with prediction)
      - Curve → missing nonlinear term
      - Offset → bias (model systematically over/under-predicts)
    - Residuals vs features:
        - Trend in residuals vs hour → missing time-of-day effect
      - Cluster in residuals → subgroup model misses
    - QQ-plot of residuals:
        - Heavy tails → outliers or incorrect error distribution assumption
      - S-shaped → missing skewness in features
  - Error slicing:
    - Group by feature bins (e.g., distance 0-5km, 5-10km, etc.)
    - Report MAE/RMSE per slice; identify where model struggles
    - Example: "Error doubles for trips >20km" → suggests missing long-distance
  feature
  - Qualitative review:
    - Examine top 10 worst predictions
    - Look for patterns:
        - All airport trips? → missing zone feature
      - All rainy days? → missing weather interaction
      - All short trips with high fare? → waiting time not captured

  Classification Error Analysis

  - Confusion matrix deep dive:
    - False positives:
        - What do these instances have in common?
      - Example: All FP trips are short downtown rides → model confuses short
  trips with tipping behavior
    - False negatives:
        - What are we missing?
      - Example: All FN trips have passenger_count>4 → larger groups tip less
  but model didn't learn
  - Example-based review:
    - Examine top 10 FP and FN by prediction confidence
    - High-confidence errors often reveal systematic flaws
    - Low-confidence errors may be irreducible noise
  - Probability calibration check:
    - Bin predictions by probability (e.g., 0.6-0.7)
    - Plot mean predicted vs actual frequency in bin
    - Deviations indicate miscalibration; Platt scaling or isotonic regression
  may help

  Overfitting & Underfitting Detection

  Overfitting Signs

  - Training error << Validation error:
    - Classic sign; model memorizes training noise
    - Quantify: (Val error - Train error) / Train error > 0.3 suggests concern
  - Validation error increases with more training:
    - Especially clear in learning curves (plot error vs. training size)
    - If validation error starts rising after X epochs/rounds → stop there
  - High variance in performance across CV folds:
    - Indicates model sensitive to specific training samples
    - Especially troubling if folds are temporally ordered (suggests concept
  drift overfit)
  - Complex model performs no better than simple baseline:
    - Suggests extra capacity fitting noise, not signal
  - Remedies:
    - Increase regularization (lambda, dropout, min_samples_leaf)
    - Reduce model complexity (depth, estimators)
    - Early stopping
    - More data (if feasible)
    - Simpler feature set

  Underfitting Signs

  - Training error ≈ Validation error >> Bayes error:
    - Both high; model too simple to capture patterns
    - Quantify: If naive baseline error is close to model error → severe
  underfit
  - Learning curves:
    - Both training and validation error high and converging
    - Adding more data doesn't help (yet)
  - High bias in residuals:
    - Systematic patterns in residual plots (e.g., U-shape indicating missed
  quadratic term)
    - Feature importance shows weak signals across all features
  - Remedies:
    - Increase model complexity (depth, features)
    - Feature engineering (interactions, transforms)
    - Reduce regularization
    - Try different algorithm family (e.g., move from linear to tree-based)

  Bias-Variance Decomposition (Conceptual)

  - Expected error = Bias² + Variance + Irreducible error
  - High bias:
    - Model underfits; predictions consistently off true value
    - Fixed by: More expressive model, better features
  - High variance:
    - Model overfits; predictions change wildly with small data changes
    - Fixed by: More data, regularization, simpler model
  - Practical check:
    - If collecting more data reduces validation error → variance dominant
    - If collecting more data does nothing → bias dominant
    - In taxi data:
        - Early projects often bias-dominated (missing key features like time
  interactions)
      - Later projects may become variance-dominated (over-engineered features)

  Business-Aligned Metric Selection

  Process

  1. Define cost matrix:
    - Explicitly state business cost of each error type
    - Example for trip rejection system:
        - Cost(false positive) = $20 (lost fare + customer dissatisfaction)
      - Cost(false negative) = $5 (minor detour inefficiency)
  2. Simulate expected cost:
    - For each candidate threshold/model:
        - Expected cost = Σ [P(class) × P(error|class) × Cost(error)]
    - Choose threshold/model minimizing expected cost
  3. Validate with business KPIs:
    - If model deployed, track:
        - Actual revenue impact
      - Customer satisfaction change
      - Operational metric shift (e.g., average pickup time)
  4. Communicate in business terms:
    - Never say "AUC improved by 0.02"
    - Say: "Model reduces expected rejection cost by $0.03/trip → $12k/month
  savings"

  10. PRODUCTION-LEVEL THINKING

  Building Reusable Pipelines

  Pipeline Architecture Principles

  - Immutability:
    - Each pipeline stage produces new artifacts; never modifies input in-place
    - Enables reproducibility and debugging
  - Explicit interfaces:
    - Clear input/output schemas (e.g., "expects DataFrame with columns
  [X,Y,Z]")
    - Use schema validation (e.g., Pandera, Great Expectations) at stage
  boundaries
  - Idempotency:
    - Running same pipeline twice yields same output
    - Critical for retry safety and caching
  - Separation of concerns:
    - Data ingestion ≠ feature engineering ≠ model training ≠ evaluation
    - Each stage testable in isolation

  Scikit-Learn Pipeline Implementation

  - Preprocessing Pipeline:
    - ColumnTransformer handling:
        - Numerical: Imputer → Scaler
      - Categorical: Imputer → Encoder (OneHot/Target)
      - Datetime: Extractor → Cyclical encoder → Scaler
    - Critical:
        - Fit transformers only on training data
      - Never peek at validation/test during fitting
  - Model Pipeline:
    - Pipeline([('preprocess', preprocess_pipe), ('model', model)])
    - Enables single .fit() and .predict() calls
    - Ensures identical preprocessing at train/predict time
  - When to avoid monolithic pipeline:
    - If preprocessing very slow (e.g., geospatial enrichment)
    - Consider separate feature store with versioned features

  Saving Transformers & Model Serialization

  Transformer Persistence

  - Joblib vs Pickle:
    - Joblib: Preferred for scikit-learn objects (efficient numpy handling)
    - Pickle: Universal but slower; security risk with untrusted data
  - Versioning:
    - Save with metadata:
  metadata = {
      'version': 'v2.1',
      'features_used': ['distance', 'hour_sin', ...],
      'train_date': '2026-05-01',
      'scikit_version': '1.4.0'
  }
  joblib.dump((preprocessor, model, metadata), 'model_v2.1.joblib')

  - Validation on load:
    - Check metadata compatibility before use
    - Raise error if feature set or version mismatched

  Model-Specific Considerations

  - Tree-based models:
    - Generally serialize well; check for random state persistence
  - Linear models:
    - Coefficients stable; watch for solver convergence differences
  - Neural networks:
    - Framework-specific (TensorFlow SavedModel, PyTorch torch.save)
    - Require same framework version for loading
  - Critical test:
    - Load serialized model on clean environment
    - Verify predictions match original within tolerance

  Experiment Tracking & Model Versioning

  Tracking System Requirements

  - Immutable experiment logs:
    - Once written, experiment record cannot be altered
    - Prevents retroactive "improvement" of results
  - Searchable metadata:
    - Query by: date, feature set, hyperparameter range, metric threshold
    - Essential for model archaeology
  - Artifact storage:
    - Link to model files, plots, data slices
    - Enables full reproduction without re-running
  - Comparison tools:
    - Side-by-side metric comparison
    - Feature importance drift visualization
    - Prediction distribution comparison

  Model Versioning Strategy

  - Semantic versioning for models:
    - MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH
        - MAJOR: Architecture change (e.g., LR → GBT)
      - MINOR: Feature set change
      - PATCH: Hyperparameter tune / retrain same config
  - Staging environments:
    - Development: Latest experiment; for testing new ideas
    - Staging: Promotion criteria met; undergoes integration testing
    - Production: Currently serving traffic; only promoted after validation
  - Promotion gates:
    - To staging:
        - Passes unit tests on API contract
      - Validation metrics within X% of baseline
      - No regressions on key slices (e.g., airport trips)
    - To production:
        - Staging performance stable for 72h
      - No drift in input distribution
      - Business A/B test shows positive impact
  - Rollback capability:
    - Maintain ability to revert to previous version in <5min
    - Store previous version artifacts; not just delete

  Reproducibility Practices

  Environmental Control

  - Exact dependencies:
    - requirements.txt pinned to specific versions (not >=)
    - Include OS and Python version if critical (python==3.11.2)
  - Deterministic seeds:
    - Set numpy, random, torch, tensorflow seeds
    - Critical: Also set seeds for any library with randomness (e.g.,
  scikit-learn shuffling)
    - Verify: Same seed → same output across runs
  - Hardware consistency:
    - Note CPU/GPU model if using GPU-sensitive ops
    - Prefer CPU for reproducibility unless GPU essential
  - Data freezing:
    - Hash of input data saved with experiment
    - Never re-run with "latest data" without explicit versioning

  Protocol for Reproducibility Claims

  - To claim "reproducible":
    a. Same code version (git hash)
    b. Same data version (input hash)
    c. Same environment (dependencies + OS)
    d. Same seeds
    e. Same command → same output
  - Documentation:
    - README must specify:
        - Exact reproduction command
      - Expected runtime
      - Known non-deterministic sources (if any)
      - Tolerance for floating point differences
  - When full reproducibility impossible:
    - Acknowledge sources of non-determinism (e.g., thread scheduling)
    - Report distribution of outcomes over 10 runs
    - Focus on reproducibility of conclusions, not bit-for-bit output

  Deployment Readiness

  API Design Considerations

  - Input contract:
    - Strict schema validation (e.g., Pydantic, JSON Schema)
    - Reject malformed requests early with clear error codes
    - Example:
  {
    "required": ["pickup_lat", "pickup_lon", "dropoff_lat", "dropoff_lon",
  "timestamp"],
    "properties": { "passenger_count": {"minimum":1, "maximum":6} }
  }
  - Output contract:
    - Consistent structure:
  {
    "prediction": 12.45,
    "prediction_units": "USD",
    "confidence_interval": [11.20, 13.70],
    "model_version": "fare_predictor_v2.1",
    "timestamp": "2026-05-12T10:30:00Z"
  }
  - Error handling:
    - Distinguish client errors (4xx) from server errors (5xx)
    - Log server errors with traceback; never expose internals to client
  - Performance:
    - Target latency: 95th percentile <100ms for real-time
    - Monitor: Request rate, latency, error rate, CPU/memory

  Containerization & Infrastructure

  - Dockerfile essentials:
    - Multi-stage build to minimize image size
    - Non-root user for security
    - Health check endpoint (/health)
    - Expose only necessary ports
  - Configuration management:
    - Environment-specific config (dev/stage/prod)
    - Secrets never in image; use vault or sealed secrets
    - Feature flags for safe rollout
  - Scalability:
    - Stateless preferred; store state in external store (Redis, DB)
    - Horizontal pod autoscaling based on CPU/request rate
    - Circuit breaker for downstream dependencies
  - Observability:
    - Structured logging (JSON) to central system
    - Key metrics: latency, error rate, prediction drift
    - Distributed tracing for request journeys

  Monitoring Strategy

  Data Drift Detection

  - Input feature drift:
    - Population Stability Index (PSI):
        - PSI = Σ [(actual_% - expected_%) × ln(actual_% / expected_%)]
      - PSI > 0.2: Significant drift; >0.5: Severe drift
    - Kolmogorov-Smirnov test:
        - Non-parametric; detects distribution shifts
      - Sensitive to sample size; use with effect size
    - When to trigger:
        - PSI > 0.3 on >20% of features → investigate data source
      - Sudden shift in key feature (e.g., average trip distance drops 30%)
  - Concept drift detection:
    - Windowed performance:
        - Track rolling window of prediction error (e.g., last 1h)
      - Compare to baseline performance
      - Significant increase → model stale
    - Drift detection methods:
        - ADWIN, DDM, EDDM (for streaming)
      - Simple: Compare validation error on recent vs. historical data
  - Response protocol:
    - Level 1 (Warning): PSI 0.1-0.2 → alert data engineering
    - Level 2 (Investigation): PSI >0.2 or perf drop >10% → root cause analysis
    - Level 3 (Action): Perf drop >20% → trigger retrain or rollback

  Model Performance Monitoring

  - Ground truth lag handling:
    - Acknowledge delay in getting true labels (e.g., tips settled weekly)
    - Use proxy metrics when available (e.g., driver earnings per hour)
    - Monitor: Prediction stability (std of predictions over time)
  - Key metrics to track:
    - Prediction distribution:
        - Mean, std, skewness of predictions
      - Shift indicates input drift or model change
    - Error metrics:
        - MAE/RMSE on available ground truth (lagged)
      - Compare to service level objectives (SLAs)
    - Business impact:
        - If predicting tip: Actual tip rate vs predicted
      - If predicting demand: Actual trips vs forecasted trips
  - Alerting thresholds:
    - Critical:
        - MAE > 2× baseline MAE
      - Prediction mean outside 3σ of historical range
      - Business KPI deviation >15% from forecast
    - Warning:
        - MAE > 1.5× baseline
      - Prediction std changing rapidly
      - Feature missing rate >5%

  Feedback Loop for Retraining

  - Retrain triggers:
    - Time-based: Nightly/weekly if data stabilizes quickly
    - Performance-based: Validation MAE > threshold for 24h
    - Data-based: New data volume >20% since last train
    - Drift-based: PSI >0.3 on critical features
  - Retrain protocol:
    - Data window:
        - Fixed window (e.g., last 90 days) or expanding window
      - Avoid ancient data if concept drift suspected
    - Validation:
        - Must hold out most recent data for honest assessment
      - Never train and validate on same time period
    - Deployment:
        - Shadow mode first: Run new model alongside old; compare predictions
      - A/B test: 5% traffic to new model; measure business impact
      - Full rollout only if non-inferior/superior

  - Deliverables:
    - One-page problem statement
    - Data quality initial assessment
    - List of open questions for SME
  - Gate criteria:
    - Business objective documented and agreed
    - Data covers required time period and geographic scope
    - No show-stopper data issues (e.g., 100% missing target)

  Phase 2: Data Cleaning (Days 4-8)

  - Goals:
    - Produce analysis-ready dataset
    - Document all transformations
    - Quantify impact of cleaning
  - Activities:
    - Automated quality scanning (missing, duplicates, ranges)
    - Collaborative validation with SME on flagged records
    - Implement cleaning scripts with logging
    - Before/after analysis on key distributions
    - Outlier investigation and handling decision
  - Deliverables:
    - Cleaned dataset (versioned)
    - Data cleaning report
    - Data quality scorecard (pre/post)
    - Cleaning pipeline (reusable script)
  - Gate criteria:
    - <1% missing in critical features
    - No invalid values in key columns (fares, distances, times)
    - Documentation complete for all transformations
    - SME sign-off on cleaning logic

  Phase 3: Exploratory Data Analysis (Days 9-15)

  - Goals:
    - uncover patterns
    - generate feature hypotheses
    - assess relationships with target
  - Activities:
    - Univariate analysis (distributions, outliers)
    - Bivariate analysis (target correlations, group comparisons)
    - Multivariate analysis (interactions, clustering)
    - Business insight workshops
    - Visualization dashboard creation
  - Deliverables:
    - EDA report (interactive + static)
    - List of top 10 feature hypotheses with rationale
    - Business insights summary
    - Feature prioritization matrix (impact vs. effort)
  - Gate criteria:
    - At least 3 actionable feature hypotheses identified
    - Clear relationship established between features and target
    - No major surprises requiring problem reframing
    - EDA reviewed by SME and ML lead

  Phase 4: Feature Engineering (Days 16-22)

  - Goals:
    - build feature set
    - avoid leakage
    - create interpretable features
  - Activities:
    - Implement baseline features (time, distance, basic encodings)
    - Create domain-driven features (zone-based, temporal)
    - Engineer interaction features based on EDA
    - Build feature store with versioning
    - Validate feature stability (train/test PSI)
    - Document all features in catalog
  - Deliverables:
    - Feature engineering documentation
    - Trained feature pipeline (reusable)
    - Feature importance from baseline model
    - List of features dropped due to leakage/risk
    - Feature set ready for modeling
  - Gate criteria:
    - All features pass leakage check (trained only on past data)
    - <5% of features have PSI>0.2 between train/test
    - Feature documentation complete
    - Baseline model performance established

  Phase 5: Modeling (Days 23-30)

  - Goals:
    - Train and validate candidate models
    - Select final model
    - Establish baseline performance
  - Activities:
    - Implement baseline models (linear, tree)
    - Define train/validation/test split (temporal)
    - Hyperparameter tuning for top 2 algorithm families
    - Cross-validated evaluation with statistical testing
    - Error analysis and bias/variance diagnosis
    - Model selection business case
  - Deliverables:
    - Model comparison report
    - Final trained pipeline (model + preprocess)
    - Experiment tracking log
    - Model card documenting performance and limitations
    - Prediction vs actual plots for key segments
  - Gate criteria:
    - Final model meets business metric threshold (e.g., RMSE<$2.0)
    - Performance stable across temporal validation folds
    - No critical failure modes identified in error analysis
    - Model exceeds naive baseline by meaningful margin
    - SME and ML lead agree on selection

  Phase 6: Evaluation (Days 31-35)

  - Goals:
    - rigorously assess model
    - prepare for deployment
    - quantify business impact
  - Activities:
    - Full test set evaluation (holdout, never touched during training)
    - Business impact simulation (using cost matrix)
    - Calibration analysis (if probabilistic outputs needed)
    - Latency and throughput benchmarking
    - Sensitivity analysis (feature importance, SHAP values)
    - Deployment readiness checklist
  - Deliverables:
    - Final evaluation report
    - Business impact estimate
    - Performance benchmark sheet
    - Deployment readiness sign-off
    - Monitoring plan draft
  - Gate criteria:
    - Test set performance within 5% of validation estimate
    - Business impact estimate exceeds minimum threshold
    - All non-functional requirements met (latency, security, etc.)
    - Monitoring plan approved by ops team

  Phase 7: Optimization (Days 36-40)

  - Goals:
    - refine model
    - reduce complexity
    - prepare for efficient deployment
  - Activities:
    - Feature importance-driven pruning
    - Try simpler model families (e.g., linear vs tree)
    - Quantize/compress model if needed
    - Optimize feature pipeline for latency
    - A/B test design (if applicable)
    - Document known limitations and failure modes
  - Deliverables:
    - Optimized model pipeline
    - Latency/benchmark report
    - Model limitations document
    - A/B test protocol (if used)
    - Final model version for promotion
  - Gate criteria:
    - Latency meets SLA (e.g., P95<50ms)
    - Performance non-inferior to unoptimized model
    - All known failure modes documented and mitigated
    - Optimization does not introduce new failure modes

  Phase 8: Deployment Preparation (Days 41-45)

  - Goals:
    - enable safe deployment
    - establish monitoring
    - knowledge transfer
  - Activities:
    - Create deployment artifacts (Dockerfile, manifests)
    - Implement monitoring dashboards and alerts
    - Run deployment playbook in staging
    - Conduct knowledge transfer sessions
    - Create runbook for common issues
    - Final security and compliance review
  - Deliverables:
    - Deployment-ready artifact bundle
    - Monitoring dashboard (live in staging)
    - Alerting rules configured
    - Runbook for operators
    - Knowledge transfer completion record
    - Sign-off from DevOps, Security, and Business
  - Gate criteria:
    - Successful staging deployment (no critical errors)
    - Monitoring shows expected behavior
    - All documentation complete and reviewed
    - Business owner signs off on release

  13. MENTORSHIP GUIDANCE

  Common Beginner Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

  ❌ Mistake: Skipping or rushing EDA

  - Why it happens: Eager to build models; sees EDA as "boring"
  - Consequence:
    - Builds complex models on flawed assumptions
    - Misses critical data quality issues
    - Engineers features that leak or are meaningless
  - How to avoid:
    - Timebox EDA: Minimum 40% of project time
    - Deliverable-driven: Require EDA report before modeling
    - Pair with SME: Have domain expert review plots and interpretations
    - Hypothesis log: Document every insight as "If [X], then [Y] implies
  [action]"

  ❌ Mistake: Target leakage in features

  - Why it happens:
    - Using future information (e.g., dropoff_time to predict pickup_duration)
    - Creating features from target components (e.g., fare_per_mile when
  predicting fare)
    - Inadequate temporal splitting
  - Consequence:
    - Inflated validation performance
    - Model fails catastrophically in production
  - How to avoid:
    - Leakage checklist:
        - ❌ Any feature using data after prediction time
      - ❌ Any feature that is mathematical function of target
      - ❌ Any feature requiring knowledge not available at prediction time
    - Temporal split first: Define train/val/test cutoff before any feature
  engineering
    - Proxy test: Train model to predict known constant (should fail); if it
  succeeds → leakage

  ❌ Mistake: Over-engineering features

  - Why it happens:
    - Belief that more features = better model
    - Not understanding diminishing returns
    - Enjoyment of clever transformations
  - Consequence:
    - Increased complexity without gain
    - Higher maintenance burden
    - Risk of overfitting to noise
  - How to avoid:
    - Start minimal: 5-7 features max for first model
    - Feature budget: Allocate "feature points"; complex features cost more
    - Prove value: Only add feature if it improves validation metric by >0.5%
    - Kill your darlings: Remove favorite feature if it doesn't help

  ❌ Mistake: Ignoring business context in metrics

  - Why it happens:
    - Optimizing for accuracy/AUC without cost consideration
    - Not translating model output to business decisions
    - Assuming higher metric = better business outcome
  - Consequence:
    - Model optimized for wrong objective
    - Stakeholders lose trust when predictions don't improve KPIs
  - How to avoid:
    - Cost matrix workshop: Early and often with stakeholders
    - Metric mapping: Show how RMSE/MAE maps to business cost
    - Decision simulation: "If we use this model, what action do we take and
  what is outcome?"
    - Track business KPIs: Not just model metrics in production

  ❌ Mistake: Underestimating data preparation time

  - Why it happens:
    - Hollywood portrayal of ML as model-tuning montage
    - Forgetting that real data is messy
    - Assuming "clean data" exists somewhere
  - Consequence:
    - Project timeline blown
    - Rushed cleaning → quality issues
    - Blaming model for data problems
  - How to avoid:
    - Rule of thumb: 60% time on data (understanding + cleaning + EDA)
    - Track actual time: Compare estimated vs actual for data tasks
    - Celebrate cleaning wins: "Removed 200k duplicate trips" is an achievement
    - Involve SME early: They know where bodies are buried

  What to Prioritize First

  1. Business problem clarity:
    - Without this, you're optimizing for nothing
    - Spend time until you can explain the project to a smart outsider in 60
  seconds
  2. Data quality assessment:
    - Garbage in, gospel out
    - Know your data's flaws before trusting any model
  3. Simple baseline model:
    - Establishes what's learnable from the data
    - Prevents overfitting to noise via excessive complexity
  4. Validation strategy integrity:
    - If your validation leaks, everything after is fiction
    - Get this right before tuning a single hyperparameter

  What Usually Consumes Most Project Time

  - Data-related tasks (60-70%):
    - Understanding context and limitations (20%)
    - Cleaning and quality assurance (25%)
    - Exploratory analysis and feature engineering (15-25%)
  - Modeling and tuning (20-30%):
    - Baseline to advanced model progression (10%)
    - Hyperparameter tuning and validation (10-15%)
    - Error analysis and interpretation (5-10%)
  - Deployment and monitoring (10-15%):
    - Making model usable in production (5-10%)
    - Setting up feedback loops and monitoring (5%)

  How to Think Like a Real Data Scientist

  - Curious skeptic:
    - Question everything: "Why is this column here? What could be wrong with
  it?"
    - Assume data is guilty until proven innocent
    - Look for anomalies that tell stories (not just noise to remove)
  - Business translator:
    - Constantly ask: "How would this change a decision?"
    - Speak in terms of impact, not algorithms
    - Translate model outputs into actions
  - Process engineer:
    - Build repeatable, auditable workflows
    - Document assumptions and limitations
    - Design for failure (what happens when data changes?)
  - Evidence-based:
    - Let data overturn hypotheses (don't torture data to confirm beliefs)
    - Require proof before accepting claims
    - Update beliefs proportionally to evidence
  - Lazy optimizer:
    - Seek simplest solution that works
    - Avoid premature optimization
    - Automate the repetitive parts

  Documenting Findings Properly

  The "So What?" Test

  - Every insight, feature, or model choice must answer:
    - "If this is true, what would we do differently?"
    - If no action follows, it's trivia, not insight

  Documentation Layers

  1. Lab notebook (temporary):
    - Raw exploration; date-stamped
    - Includes dead ends and failed ideas
  2. Technical documentation (permanent):
    - What was done and why
    - Assumptions, limitations, validation
    - Enough for competent practitioner to reproduce
  3. Executive summary (business-facing):
    - What was learned
    - What actions are recommended
    - Estimated impact and confidence
  4. Runbook (operational):
    - How to use/maintain the solution
    - Troubleshooting common issues
    - Escalation paths

  Key Documentation Principles

  - Assume future amnesia:
    - Write for yourself 6 months in the future (or a colleague)
    - Include context: "We did X because Y"
  - Decision log:
    - Record not just what was chosen, but rejected alternatives and why
    - Example: "Rejected neural net because: 1) <50k samples 2) needs
  interpretability"
  - Error catalog:
    - Maintain list of known failure modes with triggers and fixes
    - Example: "If pickup_lat > 90, model returns NaN → fix: clip to [-90,90]
  before predict"
  - Version everything:
    - Code (git), data (hash), model (metadata), documentation (date)

  Making This Project Resume-Worthy

  Beyond "Built a Model"

  - Quantify business impact:
    - ❌ "Built a fare prediction model"
    - ✅ "Developed fare prediction model reducing RMSE by 18% ($1.20/trip),
  enabling dynamic pricing projected to increase annual revenue by $2.3M"
  - Highlight scale and complexity:
    - Mention data volume, feature count, latency constraints
    - Example: "Engineered 47 features from 12TB of trip data; model serves 5k
  RPM with P95 latency <80ms"
  - Show problem-solving process:
    - Describe key challenges overcome
    - Example: "Solved temporal leakage issue by implementing expanding window
  validation, preventing 22% overoptimism in initial estimates"
  - Emphasize transferable skills:
    - Feature engineering creativity
    - Validation rigor
    - Stakeholder communication
    - Production mindset
  - Include artifacts:
    - Link to GitHub repo (with documentation)
    - Share model card or one-pager
    - If possible, describe how solution is being used

  [Action verb] + [What you built] + [Using what methods] +
  [Quantifiable business/technical result] +
  [Context of scale/complexity]

  engineering
  to reduce prediction error by 1.8 RMSE units (22%), enabling dynamic pricing

  dataset."

  - The best data scientists spend more time questioning answers than answering

  - A model that solves the wrong problem brilliantly is still a failure
  - Your greatest asset is not your technical skill—it's your ability to learn 
  what matters
